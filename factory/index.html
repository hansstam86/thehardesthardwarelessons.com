The factory tour is not the factory

Every hardware founder remembers their first factory tour. The lobby is impressive, the SMT line is photogenic, the operators work in synchronised quiet, and the conference room has fruit on the table. Everything you saw was real, and almost nothing you saw was information.

You do not know the line's real first-pass yield, how many operators joined in the last ninety days, whether the inspector can hold a shipment on the last day of the month, or what the defect rate on the slide was calculated from. The tour shows that the factory can look like a good factory. It cannot show whether it is one.

This book is about knowing where to look.

"A factory does not add quality to your product. It reveals the quality of what you brought it."

The lessons, in compressed form

Each chapter closes with the specific mistake most teams make in that area, and what it costs. A few lines from the first five:

Chapter 1 · The Factory

"Your product's biggest factory risk is not technical. It is being a low-priority customer and not knowing it, and being told only what the factory chooses to tell you, and mistaking that for knowledge."

Chapter 2 · Receiving

"The dock is the cheapest place in the entire system, cheaper than the line, vastly cheaper than the field, to discover that one of your components is wrong."

Chapter 3 · The Warehouse

"The warehouse fails in silence and bills in arrears."

Chapter 4 · SMT

"The yield you are shown is the yield after rework. The machines already log the truth: demand the primary data, and read it."

Chapter 5 · Injection Molding

""The molder is our supplier's problem" is the most expensive sentence in mechanical manufacturing."

One system, two books

The first book follows the development journey from prototype through EVT, DVT and PVT to the gates of mass production. This one starts on the other side of the gate. Development decisions become factory outcomes: the board layout chosen in EVT becomes an SMT yield number, and the enclosure frozen at tooling release becomes a molding defect rate. You do not need to have read book one, but the two describe one connected system.

Who it's for. Hardware founders, product and operations leads, and engineers who are about to audit a factory, negotiate a manufacturing agreement, fight a yield problem, or find that a long-running relationship is drifting. Examples come mainly from Chinese contract manufacturing (Shenzhen, Dongguan and the Pearl River Delta), with excursions to Vietnam, Thailand, India and Mexico. The mechanics transfer.
